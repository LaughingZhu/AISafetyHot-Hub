## Study: online RL behavior attribution signals may be confounded by gradient magnitude and fluency

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10422) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/tf5cngqw2nj2565sglybbbtm9)

A paper studies whether behavior attribution is trustworthy on GRPO online RL fine-tuning, using controlled experiments with behaviors of known cause, and releases an open-source evaluation framework, BehaviorTrace. Across three random seeds on Qwen2.5-1.5B, a substantial portion of attribution signal comes from confounds: a control method that ranks training steps only by gradient magnitude with no behavior target reaches 4.2 to 4.5 times random level, and matches or exceeds the best directed estimator on two of three seeds. At saturated checkpoints, model fluency predicts behavior labels no worse than any compared gradient method; after controlling for fluency, per-rollout results vary across seeds and across generation samples, so a single run is inconclusive. The authors state the only signal that holds across all three seeds is alignment of the trigger token's gradient with a target constructed at the location where the behavior actually occurs. The paper tests existing methods including GAS (renormalized TracInCP) and TRAK-style estimators, proposes no new estimator, and organizes the conclusions into a checklist for evaluating RL attribution.

### Questions

Online RL may teach behaviors that were not requested, but gradient attribution has mostly been validated only in static supervised settings; whether it can localize the rollout responsible for the behavior is unclear.

### Methods

BehaviorTrace implants an early bonus on GRPO for Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, and compares GAS, TRAK-style, TracInCP against gradient norm and fluency controls using a full-gradient CountSketch.

### Experiments and results

Across three seeds, the untargeted gradient magnitude ranking reaches the abstract's claimed 4.2 to 4.5 times random and is not lower than directed estimators on two seeds. The authors state that within the training window, all methods fall near baseline rates. After controlling for fluency, which of direction or magnitude is higher flips across seeds. Trigger token gradients and in-context targets align across three seeds.

### Limitations and future work

The authors limit to Qwen2.5 up to 1.5B, GRPO only, newly generated sibling samples at step 100, and fluency control at the rollout level only for GAS. The behavior is reinforced by RL from a 10-example SFT seed; whether a behavior spread across the whole answer still has a token-level signal is called an open question by the authors.

## The paper proposes a retrieval admissibility verification framework for long-term memory agents.

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07309) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/exzp15zm6i2zfalgju2km6c5s)

The paper proposes a retrieval admissibility verification framework that labels each memory-query pair as admissible, inadmissible, or undecided, and compares different retrieval paths under matched required-evidence recall while tracking memory ID flow in prompt exposure. The authors conduct a post-hoc top-20 reanalysis of frozen rankings on two public long-term memory benchmarks, RHELM and MemOps, covering 3,767 queries: all published anchors fall within the trusted query namespace, filtering out-of-namespace content does not lower their rank when in-namespace scores are unchanged, top-20 anchor recall rises from 0.432 to 0.533, 80% recall feasibility rises from 0.237 to 0.311, and exact similarity evaluations decrease by 98.3%. In 72 frozen development diagnostics, a reference scheme based on published metadata preserves required evidence, while two text-based verifiers fail to detect violations under a 1% required-anchor false-rejection limit. The authors observe only one 95% confidence interval not containing zero among four readers across 16 controlled exposure scenarios.

### Questions

Long-term memory agents can retrieve records that are topically relevant but belong to other subjects, violate policy, or are incompatible with the lifecycle. Recall and answer accuracy do not reveal whether the record can enter the current prompt.

### Methods

The authors label memory-query pairs as admissible, inadmissible, or undecided, compare paths at the recall of matched required evidence, provide bounds for undecided labels, and then track prompt exposure and answer disclosure by memory ID. Support, eligibility, and exposure are verified by subpopulation.

### Experiments and results

Across 3,767 queries, namespace top-20 recall goes from 0.432 to 0.533. On 1,523 examples, three readers' accuracy gains are 0.053–0.068, which the authors state are observational. Across 16 scenarios, only DeepSeek V4 Pro's inadmissible disclosure interval excludes 0.

### Limitations and future work

The authors state the evidence covers only two public sources and seven RHELM groups, the namespace is a trusted label rather than inferred, and frozen-route comparisons are associational. Experiments include 3,767 queries, 1,523 paired examples, 72 fixed-candidate analyses, and a 200-output alternative-judge audit.

## Research: fact-checking evaluations may mix in future evidence unknowable at the time of the claim

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04888) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/of8dpno9hcfswb26ubo3vxvru)

Researchers reconstructed the evidence conditions at the time of claim creation on AVeriTeC and QuanTemp++, and probed model representations for encoded outcome information, finding clear parametric leakage that is masked by overall accuracy. The authors state that a simple representation bottleneck reduces such future information leakage more effectively than mutual information-based training penalties. The study also found that allowing evidence published after the claim inflates AVeriTeC zero-shot accuracy by 6.3 percentage points, while the effect is negligible on QuanTemp++, where retrieval provides almost no post-hoc evidence. The authors conclude that misinformation benchmarks overestimate fact-checking performance if they do not account for information actually available at the time of the claim.

### Questions

Fact-checking benchmarks often count information unknowable at the time of the claim as success. The parameter channel is the outcome memorized during pretraining; the temporal channel is retrieved evidence published after the claim. The authors argue that a single accuracy score cannot distinguish these two paths.

### Methods

Recover the publication date on AVeriTeC and QuanTemp++, split into point-in-time and leaked views. Entity obfuscation tests the parameter channel, with the control being GPT-2-XL truncated at December 2017. On frozen Qwen3-14B, compare width bottleneck against CLUB penalty.

### Experiments and results

Qwen3-14B after occlusion: Supported recall 0.421→0.131, accuracy drops 0.032; control reverses. AVeriTeC post-hoc evidence raises zero-shot by 6.3 points; with prior evidence +0.031. QuanTemp++ difference 0.05% (p=0.87).

### Limitations and future work

The authors state that outcome information cannot be said to be completely removed: date coverage is incomplete, no manual spot checks were done, and the two date tools differ. The training detector uses only Qwen3-14B, with features processed via mean-pooling and PCA. Next, they will test claims after the training cutoff and check whether high-quality evidence exists before the claim.

## Persist-3 uses sequential testing to identify unseen jailbreak sources, improving the defense effectiveness of MTK and Prompt Guard 2

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09167) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/mek7qa97peombxtebbf9oum6y)

The paper proposes Persist-3 to determine, from a stream of attack prompts, whether they come from a source outside the known attack sources. The method computes a conformal p-value for each known source and rejects the null hypothesis only when the evidence against all known sources is strong enough. Theoretical analysis shows that its detection probability approaches 1 exponentially as the observation window grows, so a shorter window suffices to detect unseen sources that are clearly distinguishable from known ones. The authors validate Persist-3 on top of two recent jailbreak defenses, MTK and Prompt Guard 2, and observe a significant enhancement in defense effectiveness.

### Questions

Jailbreak defenses are usually calibrated on fixed known attack methods, and existing detectors degrade when new methods appear. The authors ask: looking only at the representation stream before the victim model returns a response, how can one tell that the current attack does not belong to any known source, and how does the degree of separation determine detection speed? Switches between known sources should not count as targets.

### Methods

For each known source, compute a Mahalanobis score and a split conformal p-value. Persist-3 averages over the recent W=10 per source, then takes the minimum, and requires L=3 consecutive threshold crossings, so it depends only on the last 12 observations. Thresholds use only known-source validation streams. After alarm, escalate the stream on the unmodified guardrail.

### Experiments and results

At H=1000, Persist-3's KU detection rate is 0.458, SR-DKW full is 0.648, and stationary FPR is 0/1835 for both (Table 1). Layered on MTK or PG2, the authors state block rate increases by 7.0 to 10.0 percentage points (Table 2).

### Limitations and future work

The authors list asymptotic analysis under more online data as a future direction, and state explicitly that fully adaptive attackers are not covered by the theorems or stress tests. Empirically, there is no registration after verified alarms; stratified comparisons are not budget-matched. Replay is not compared with the stream-level defenses of Piet et al. (2025).

## Research: An agent stopping on time does not equal productive use of its time budget

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10833) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/a96zojasssfyepiv1oigbpqb5)

The paper studies whether small LLM agents can both adhere to a runtime and allocate time productively under an explicit wall-clock budget; it evaluates Qwen3.6-27B on five tasks from MLE-Bench Lite and Qwen3-4B on Zork I (Jericho). When the budget is written only into the prompt, the agent cannot convert the budget into controlled time use, due to a lack of time perception, difficulty in estimating action durations, and no learned mapping from available time to policy. The authors test two types of intervention: exposing time information via a harness and enforcing deadlines, and reinforcement learning with budget-aware rewards. Harness-injected time information significantly improves budget adherence for Qwen3.6-27B without a measurable performance loss; enforcement hooks further improve adherence; GRPO reinforcement learning attains near-perfect budget adherence on Zork I and generalizes to budgets unseen during training, but it does not improve task performance on MLE-Bench.

### Questions

Agents need to return within an explicit wall-clock budget and, on search-type tasks, trade more time for better solutions. The authors divide the requirement into adherence (returning on time) and productive use (more time improving results). When only written in the prompt, the model has no timing feedback and cannot reliably estimate action durations.

### Methods

Across five MLE-Bench (Qwen3.6-27B) and Zork I (Qwen3-4B), compare prompt budgets, clock harnesses that can truncate or refuse timed-out commands, and GRPO. Zork's reward adds a time term to the score, and exceeding 1.1x the budget can zero the entire segment.

### Experiments and results

Table 2: Qwen3.6-27B, 15 minutes, prompt-only on-time rate 15%, refuse 88%, clock time used 99.7% of budget. Qwen3-4B, RL+Auto-Clock, 16 seconds at 46.4/350. The authors say it will stop, but adding time did not improve scores.

### Limitations and future work

The authors note that policy collapse, memorization, and evaluation scope remain open, and suggest exploring reward and environment changes. Experiments are five MLE-Bench tasks (Qwen3.6-27B) and Zork I; Table 6 has n=16 per cell. The paper does not report the number of episodes for Table 2.

## Opera: A Verbal Critic Framework for Long-Horizon Coding Agents

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33987) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/lytkh18wwq0isocm0bhktp3ov)

The researchers propose Opera, a verbal critic framework for long-horizon coding agents, that tracks each correction as a persistent note until the diagnosed problem is truly resolved. It decides when to review via periodic and event-driven triggers, diagnoses problems with typed operators, audits feedback against visible evidence before delivery, and tracks the agent's subsequent actions to distinguish superficial compliance from actual resolution. As a test-time critic, Opera improves the resolve rate of non-critic agents by up to 12.4, 15.0, and 8.9 percentage points on Terminal-Bench 2.1, a SWE-Bench Pro subset, and DeepSWE v1.1 respectively, across four policy models, and achieves the highest average resolve rate among competitive critic baselines on all three benchmarks, while also providing gains when the policy model self-criticizes. Code is open-sourced at https://github.com/dongyuanjushi/Opera。

### Questions

Long-horizon coding agents often repeat failures or report completion without verification, and feedback that misjudges incomplete work can be harmful. Existing critics rarely track whether the problem is actually resolved after feedback.

### Methods

Opera turns each correction into a persistent note: periodic or five event types trigger review, nine contract-bearing operators diagnose only one issue each time, delivery only after admission audit passes, and tracks compliance and resolution separately.

### Experiments and results

Under GPT-5.6-Sol criticism, Qwen3.5-9B's resolve rate improves by 12.4 and 15.0 percentage points on TB2.1 and SWE-Pro. Table 1's DeepSWE gain is 8.9 percentage points. Qwen3.5-9B solves no tasks on DeepSWE.

### Limitations and future work

The conclusion states that training comes from single-model supervised fine-tuning and one out-of-domain benchmark, and future work could expand data, iterate with post-training models, or combine with reinforcement learning. Experiments include three benchmarks and four policy models; Table 1 does not state the number of repetitions.

## Paper uses generative agents to model how private test sets affect the gap between scores and user experience

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09296) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/fw1b4bwqs4596pq2vfdhzvbro)

An arXiv paper proposes a simulation architecture combining rule-based market dynamics with LLM-driven policy agents to study how benchmark design affects decisions by model providers, users, funders, and regulators. The authors model benchmarks, consumer demand, and provider capabilities as vectors over six capability dimensions: reasoning, programming, knowledge, safety, communication, and agents, and use benchmark held-out set design as a case study. The simulation finds that shifting from public benchmarks to private held-out benchmarks narrows the gap between scores and user satisfaction on most benchmarks, but widens it on a few, depending on which capabilities the held-out weights attribute scores to. The authors say the simulation can serve as a hypothesis-generation sandbox for studying how evaluation and policy choices reshape the ecosystem in turn.

### Questions

Benchmark scores guide R&D, capital, and regulation, but design methods often fix the rest of the ecosystem. The authors argue that evaluation should be treated as a closed loop, asking what the ecosystem does after a benchmark exists, using whether the holdout set is public as a case study.

### Methods

The authors build a hybrid generative multi-agent model. A six-dimensional vector represents capabilities, benchmark loadings, and consumer demand; LLMs only make monthly institutional allocations, while scoring and market use rules, and information is forced into three parts: public, private, and truth. The case compares five holdout settings.

### Experiments and results

The authors report that from fully public to fully private, the absolute gap in LLM runs narrowed in 10 of 13 benchmarks and widened in 3, and this held for 10/10 seeds; for the heuristic it narrowed in 9/13. The authors state that widening occurred when the held-out weights shifted scoring credit toward dimensions where R&D was already concentrated.

### Limitations and future work

The authors state that the mechanism depends on weight perturbation, a six-dimensional ontology, leaderboard competition, and model-defined satisfaction; dimension loadings are settings. The simulation does not predict real markets and needs to be tested on real leaderboards. The heuristic uses 50 seeds per condition, Sonnet 10, and Opus and GPT-5.5 each 3.

## FAITH: A feasibility-aware safety filtering reinforcement learning framework for high-dimensional systems

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12432) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/d4pxfhyhnqsfgy3qgltwlqu7h)

FAITH is a model-free, feasibility-aware safe filtering reinforcement learning framework that uses a feedforward network to approximate the optimal state-action safety value and amortizes minimal intervention filtering, allowing the task policy to optimize reward through the filtered dynamics without adding competing safety terms in policy updates. On a double integrator, Safety Gym, and a 29-DoF humanoid robot, FAITH achieves no violations and the highest reward from feasible starting points, reaching a 99.95% safety rate in Walking-Avoid while retaining 97% of the unfiltered reward, and achieving the highest safety rate in Push-Avoid by sacrificing balance and falling away from the protected zone; the same policy is also demonstrated on a real Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

### Questions

Safe reinforcement learning often places safety and task return in the same policy objective, and updates may cancel each other out. Classical filters require analytic safety functions and dynamics and only minimize instantaneous action deviation; projection is undefined when no safe action exists. High-dimensional contact systems also need to distinguish whether violations can ultimately be avoided.

### Methods

FAITH uses a backup actor and safety critic to approximate the optimal state-action peak harm, then amortizes minimal intervention with a feedforward residual network. The task policy uses PPO to maximize task reward only on filtered dynamics. When the acceptance set is empty, the same loss prioritizes lower predicted peak harm, without switching to a recovery controller.

### Experiments and results

On feasible starting points in the double integrator and Safety Gym, FAITH achieves 100% safety rate, and the authors claim the highest reward among violation-free methods. Walking-Avoid safety rate 99.95%, the authors claim retaining 97% of unfiltered reward; Push-Avoid safety rate 85.45%, the highest measured.

### Limitations and future work

The authors note that with discounting, finite λ, and learning errors, there are no formal safety or optimality guarantees; evaluation is limited to planar protected zones, excluding impact severity and human injury. Hardware demonstrations are qualitative, with no safety or harm statistics. Simulation covers the double integrator, SafetyCarCircle1-v0, and two 29-DoF tasks.
