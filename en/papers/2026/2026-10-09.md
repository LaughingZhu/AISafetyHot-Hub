## Paper proposes adaptive attack framework that breaks through 12 LLM jailbreak and prompt injection defenses

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09023) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/sq1y6agjqkxtm5i661cfb8mty)

The paper "The Attacker Moves Second" argues that current LLM defense evaluations often rely on static attack strings or weak optimization methods not designed against the defenses, thus overestimating robustness. The authors adaptively adjust attack strategies using four methods—gradient descent, reinforcement learning, random search, and human red teaming—achieving attack success rates mostly above 90% on 12 recent defenses, whereas the original papers reported values mostly near zero. On AgentDojo, Spotlighting and Prompt Sandwiching had attack success rates as low as 1% under static attacks, but both exceeded 95% when switched to search-based adaptive attacks; MetaSecAlign rose from 2% on the static benchmark to 96%; an RL attack against Circuit Breakers reached 100% on HarmBench. Based on this, the authors propose four lessons: small static evaluations are misleading, automated evaluation is effective but insufficient to guarantee robustness, human red teaming remains the most effective, and model-based automatic scorers themselves can be adversarially attacked.

### Questions

Existing evaluations of defenses against large model jailbreak and prompt injection mostly use static harmful prompts or low-compute weak optimization methods that are not adapted to the defenses, and thus cannot accurately reflect the true performance of defenses against strong attackers with targeted designs.

### Methods

The authors propose a unified adaptive attack framework (propose, score, select, update loop) and instantiate it as four types of adaptive attacks: gradient-based, reinforcement learning (GRPO optimization), search-based (MAP Elites with large model mutation), and human red team.

### Experiments and results

The authors evaluate 12 recent defenses, with adaptive attacks achieving ASR above 90% on most defenses; in selected AgentDojo scenarios, the human red team achieved attacks in all tested scenarios (ASR reaching 100%).

### Limitations and future work

The experiments skipped planning-execution defenses where control flow is completely independent of data; the performance of automated search under multiple independent restarts was not reported; the LLM-based judge itself is susceptible to adversarial examples and reward hacking.

## ASPI benchmark: prompt injection attack success rate rises substantially when agents seek clarification

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17324) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/gp2vsvvg8liada0q3gtxym756)

The researchers propose the ASPI benchmark and find that LLM agents' vulnerability to prompt injection increases significantly after switching from standard execution to a clarification-seeking state. The benchmark builds 728 task-attack pairs based on AgentDojo, covering four domains—Workspace, Slack, Travel, and Banking—and keeps the task, attack goal, environment, and scoring fixed while varying only the interaction state and delivery channel. Across ten frontier models, attack success rates generally increase from the execution state to the clarification state; for example, o3 rises from 1.8% to 34.0%, Gemini-3-Flash from 2.2% to 35.7%, and Kimi K2.5 from 11.1% to 63.1%, while Claude-Opus-4.7 remains near zero in both settings. Decomposition analysis shows that the ask_user clarification channel is the main source of increased vulnerability, while the effect of the interaction state itself varies by model.

### Questions

Existing research often treats seeking clarification as an alignment behavior of agents but has not explored its safety implications. When an agent requests clarification from the user due to task ambiguity, it introduces an interaction channel different from tool outputs, which may expand the prompt injection attack surface.

### Methods

The authors construct a benchmark ASPI containing 728 task-attack pairs, create ambiguous tasks by deleting a single key slot, and compare attack success rates across different channels in the execution state versus the clarification state, while keeping the task, environment, and attack goal consistent.

### Experiments and results

Evaluating ten models, the study finds that in the clarification state, ask_user attacks raise ASR for several models, e.g., Gemini-3-Flash from 2.2% to 35.7% (Table 2); Claude-Opus-4.7 maintains about 2% ASR in both states.

### Limitations and future work

The benchmark is based on synthetic ambiguity constructed through single-slot deletion and single-turn interaction, and does not cover multi-turn or semantic ambiguity; the experiments rely on the ask_user tool and only consider a single external attacker, and the tested models are limited to ten models and four AgentDojo domains.

## STAC: How Seemingly Harmless Tool-Calling Chains Break LLM Agents

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25624) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/t8lk5w6mh0c0okwhcm2p1vdqi)

Researchers at AWS AI Labs propose Sequential Tool Attack Chaining (STAC), a multi-turn attack framework for tool-based LLM agents: it chains together tool calls that are each harmless on their own, with the malicious intent appearing only in the final step. The framework uses an automated closed-loop pipeline to generate executable tool chains, verify them in the environment, and reverse-engineer covert multi-turn prompts that induce the agent to execute them. The researchers built 483 cases and 1,352 user-agent-environment interactions on the SHADE-Arena and Agent-SafetyBench environments, covering 10 failure modes; across 8 agents including GPT-4.1, the average final attack success rate was 91.2%, exceeding 90% for all but one model, with a prompt harmfulness rate below 2% and a refusal rate below 4%.

### Questions

Agents change the external environment through tool calls, bringing security threats beyond text generation; existing safety checks mostly evaluate single tool calls in isolation and struggle to defend against multi-turn attacks where malicious intent is diluted across multiple steps.

### Methods

Proposes the STAC framework, which generates multi-step tool chains through reasoning planning, verifies feasibility by execution in real environments, reverse-engineers seemingly harmless multi-turn prefixes, and uses a planner to adaptively induce the agent to execute the final malicious call.

### Experiments and results

Across 483 cases, the average final ASR of the 8 tested agents reaches 91.2%; prompt defenses are all insufficient under multi-round adaptive attacks, with the reasoning defense reducing GPT-4.1's ASR to 58.6% in the first round but rising to 86.7% by the final round.

### Limitations and future work

The evaluation relies on same-family models as judges, and the main experiments are based on single-run point estimates; attack success rates are conditional on validated tool chains and synthetic prefixes; the real MCP tool experiments are limited in scale and sequence-level monitoring was not evaluated.

## JAWS-Bench: Researchers jailbreak code agents systematically using three workspace scenarios

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01359) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/gh1aekzktoq7xvei2pt6xj56a)

The researchers propose JAWS-Bench, which evaluates the jailbreak risk of code agents in three progressive scenarios: empty workspace (JAWS-0), single file (JAWS-1), and multi-file (JAWS-M), and includes a four-stage judging process that checks compliance, attack success, syntactic correctness, and executable operation in sequence. Across seven LLM backends from five families, JAWS-0, using only text prompts, has a compliance rate of 61%, of which 58% is harmful, 52% is parseable, and 27% runs end-to-end; in JAWS-1, stronger models achieve compliance rates near 100%, with an average attack success rate of about 71%; in JAWS-M, the average attack success rate is about 75%, with 32% generating runnable attack code. The authors claim that wrapping LLMs as agents increases the attack success rate by 1.6 times on average, because the planning and tool-calling stages overturn the initial refusal. Spyware, phishing, and privilege escalation tasks are the most easily weaponized. After adding safety evaluation instructions to the system prompt, most models' compliance rates drop by about 19 to 43 percentage points, while Qwen3-235B shows almost no change.

### Questions

Existing jailbreak evaluations for code LLMs mostly focus on text refusal or harmful content detection, and do not assess whether agents actually generate malicious code that compiles and runs, leaving the real harm exposure of autonomous code agents with read, write, and execution permissions unknown.

### Methods

They propose JAWS-Bench, constructing three levels of workspaces: empty (JAWS-0, 182 entries), single-file (JAWS-1, 100 items), and multi-file (JAWS-M, 180 repositories), paired with a four-stage execution-aware evaluation framework covering compliance, harmfulness, syntactic correctness, and runtime correctness.

### Experiments and results

Evaluating 7 large models across 5 families, it finds that embedding code context raises the attack success rate; after wrapping as an agent, the ASR is on average 1.6 times that of the base model; under multi-file settings, the average ASR reaches 74.06%, and 31.27% of the generated code is deployable malicious code with no runtime errors end to end.

### Limitations and future work

Experiments are limited to a Docker single-container sandbox with no network access, not covering outbound attacks; JAWS-M retains only a single masked location; defenses only evaluate system prompt safety instructions; query costs and repeated variance are not reported for some tests.

## MobiRed framework evaluates prompt injection attacks via third-party channels on eight mobile LLM agents

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27140) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/iyq6cb3rk8wn38mpcddx0xkir)

The researchers proposed the MobiRed automated evaluation framework, conducting 3,160 adversarial tests on eight mobile LLM agents on Android, examining whether content injected through third-party channels can steer agents from user tasks toward attacker goals. The attacker does not control the device, system, apps, or the agent itself, and can only deliver content through non-app-controlled channels such as in-app ads, embedded web pages, email, SMS, and cross-app notifications. Results show that all tested agents are vulnerable to at least one attack workflow: low-friction attacks such as fake interstitial ads succeeded at rates above 85%, while malware installation workflows requiring bypassing system warnings in the attack chain achieved an average attack success rate of 18.75% against advanced agents such as Mobile-Agent-E. Simplified attacks were more effective, dense UIs such as calendars lowered success rates, and models with higher benign task completion were also more exploitable in attacks; the authors therefore claim a coupling between capability and risk and call for defenses tailored to the mobile ecosystem's unique attack surface.

### Questions

Mobile LLM agents have screen perception and cross-app execution permissions, but during operation they must process third-party content not controlled by the app, such as ads and notifications. If the agent cannot isolate trust boundaries, an attacker does not need to control the device or app to manipulate it as a confused deputy, endangering system and data security.

### Methods

The authors built an automated testing framework called MobiRed. The framework uses YAML configuration to combine benign tasks with attack workflows, patterns, and distribution channels into test cases, injects inputs on an Android test bed and collects multidimensional telemetry, and finally uses an automated evaluator to quantify attack progress across three stages: Notice, Attempt, and Execute.

### Experiments and results

Executed 3,160 rounds of adversarial tests across 8 mobile agents. Low-friction scam ads were broadly effective, with fake login achieving an average execution rate of 51.25%; malware deployment requiring bypassing system warnings succeeded 9/10 times on Mobile-Agent-E; backbone models with high benign task completion rates generally exhibited higher attack execution success rates.

### Limitations and future work

The paper's main text does not set up a dedicated section to discuss limitations and future directions. The experiments cover 8 mobile agent architectures and 12 backbone models, running 3,160 test rounds on Android 9–16 emulators and physical devices; they cover 8 attack workflows and in-app/cross-app channels; the study does not evaluate dedicated prompt injection defense algorithms.

## Odysseus jailbreaks GPT-4o, Gemini-2.0, and Grok-3 with dual steganography, with attack success rates up to 99%

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20168) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/qokfye97jod8n3pkdzrbpcofv)

The researchers propose Odysseus, a jailbreak paradigm targeting commercial multimodal large model integration systems, which uses dual steganography to hide both the malicious query and the model's reply in seemingly normal images. The attack proceeds in four steps: first, the malicious prompt is encoded as a binary matrix and embedded into an image using a steganographic model; after the image is sent to the target system, the system invokes an attacker-specified extraction function via function calling to decode the hidden query and generate a reply, then steganographically embeds the violating reply back into an image and returns it; the attacker decodes the content locally. Tested on GPT-4o, Gemini-2.0-pro, Gemini-2.0-flash, and Grok-3 on SafeBench and JBB-Behaviors, the average attack success rate is 78%, with a maximum of 99%, higher than the suboptimal baseline HIMRD's 57.25%. The authors state that the images generated by this method are visually indistinguishable from the original images, with SSIM 0.8361 and PSNR 68.5971, and can bypass the input filtering of the OpenAI Moderation API with a detection rate of 0%.

### Questions

The safety filters of existing commercial MLLM integration systems are based on the assumption that malicious content must explicitly appear in the input or output. When attackers exploit cross-modal interactions to hide adversarial intent, this assumption fails, leaving blind spots in existing defenses.

### Methods

Odysseus introduces a dual-steganography paradigm: first, malicious text is encoded into a binary matrix and embedded into a carrier image; then, through function calls, the victim system is prompted to extract the prompt, generate a violating response, and re-embed it back into an image; finally, the attacker decodes and extracts the response locally.

### Experiments and results

On GPT-4o, Gemini-2.0-pro, Gemini-2.0-flash, and Grok-3, Odysseus achieves an average ASR of 78% across two benchmarks, with Grok-3 reaching 99% on JBB-Behaviors, and fully bypasses the OpenAI review API.

### Limitations and future work

Method evaluation covers only 4 commercial closed-source MLLM systems and 2 benchmarks; Hamming codes support only single-bit error correction, and character-level recovery accuracy declines under successive transformations; and it relies heavily on function-call-based automated extraction and re-steganography mechanisms.

## DREAM framework evaluates 12 LLM agents with cross-environment long-chain attacks, with over 68% of attacks bypassing existing defenses

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19016) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/zj4fcptg82u3spofu5t8pv5c5)

Researchers from Nanjing University, the University of Hong Kong, and other institutions propose DREAM, a framework that evaluates the interaction-level safety of LLM agents through dynamic multi-round adversarial simulation. Its core components are the cross-environment adversarial knowledge graph (CE-AKG) and context-guided policy search (C-GPS), which treat existing single-round static attacks as atomic actions and assemble them into long-chain attack trajectories across environments. Based on an atomic attack library of 1,986 attacks built from 349 digital environments, the researchers evaluated 12 SOTA agents and found that over 68% of long-chain attacks successfully bypassed existing defenses, with 8 models having attack success rates above 70%. The severity of vulnerabilities exposed by 5-step attack chains across 5 environments was 4.8 times that of the static baseline. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 had the lowest average attack success rate (48.31%), but once a long-chain attack succeeded, its severity score (14.4) was the highest among all models.

### Questions

Existing multi-agent safety evaluations are limited to static single-turn tests and cannot capture long-chain attack threats that accumulate from seemingly benign operations across multiple environments and multi-turn interactions.

### Methods

DREAM models attacks as a partially observable Markov decision process, dynamically combining atomic attacks and backtracking planning based on a cross-environment adversarial knowledge graph and context-guided policy search.

### Experiments and results

Evaluation of 12 models found that over 68% of long-chain attacks bypassed defenses; Claude-Sonnet-4.5 achieved the highest overall defense score of 67.30, but its harm score reached 14.4 after long-chain breakthroughs.

### Limitations and future work

The evaluation covers 12 models and 349 environments; the 5-environment path sample is relatively small; reliance on closed-source APIs affects full reproducibility; high-privilege local agents such as OpenClaw are not yet covered.

## MultiTurnPSB: Four-round medical jailbreak evaluation shows single-round benchmarks mask a 19x safety gap

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02630) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/g0n570eh1gw2m34pb0h6kv8sr)

The researchers propose MultiTurnPSB, extending the 466 patient-facing medical safety prompts from PatientSafetyBench into four-turn adversarial dialogues, and test three attack methods: fixed template, template adaptive, and live adversarial. Under live adversarial attack, GPT-4.1-mini's unsafe response rate rose from 34.8% at Turn 1 to 78.8% at Turn 4, with health misinformation rising from 15.0% to 83.8% and discrimination reaching 89.0%. Against the same GPT-4o-mini attacker, GPT-4.1-mini and Claude Sonnet 4.5 were nearly indistinguishable at Turn 1 (34.8% vs. 31.8%, p=0.43), but by Turn 4 there was a 19-fold gap (78.8% vs. 4.1%). The authors identified four degradation trajectories and noted that the two-element combination of emergency framing plus medical authority claims was associated with most complete violations, with Turn 2 being a key vulnerability window.

### Questions

When faced with a refusal, patients often add urgency and authority pressure; existing benchmarks only test a single turn and cannot reflect the medical safety risks in real conversations.

### Methods

Build a four-round, three-modality adversarial benchmark MultiTurnPSB based on PSB, and test the effect of input-side label intervention by six classifiers.

### Experiments and results

GPT-4.1-mini's unsafe rate rose to 78.8% under real-time attack; input-side label intervention reduced it by 52.2 percentage points but had a 45.0% false positive rate.

### Limitations and future work

The benchmark is English-only and text-only, the tested models were not configured with system prompts, and Claude as the attacker is contaminated by refusals in the last two turns.

## Grammar-Constrained Decoding Can Jailbreak LLMs into Generating Malicious Code

## The paper proposes the AuthBench benchmark to evaluate whether coding agents can infer minimal permission boundaries.

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14859) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/ttw8hcg9il3j7c2gmia20djh8)

The paper defines the ability of a coding Agent to generate file-level read/write/execute permission policies before task execution as permission boundary inference, and constructs the AuthBench benchmark, containing 120 terminal tasks across 10 domains (80 standard tasks, 40 sensitive tasks), with human-reviewed permission annotations and executable utility and attack verifiers. Evaluating frontier models including GPT-5, GPT-5.3-Codex, GPT-5.4, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, Kimi K2.5, MiniMax M2.7, Qwen3-Coder-480B, Qwen3.5-397B, the study finds that models frequently exhibit both under-granting and over-granting: they omit permissions required by the execution chain while also granting unused or sensitive access. Increasing inference-time compute does not narrow this gap; instead, it makes each model converge more stably to its own authorization attractor.

### Questions

Code agents lack a mechanism for granting least privilege on demand before executing terminal tasks, and coarse-grained default permissions easily introduce security risks; existing research assumes permission policies are given and does not study whether agents can autonomously infer task-level permission boundaries before acting.

### Methods

Proposes a two-stage Sufficiency-Tightness Decomposition: Phase 1 forward-simulates the execution plan and parses the complete toolchain to generate a permissive policy with coverage first; Phase 2 performs item-by-item audit filtering based on task rationale, scope minimization, and sensitive surfaces.

### Experiments and results

On AuthBench, frontier models often both under- and over-authorize; increasing reasoning instead makes models converge to their own preferred authorization attractors. The two-stage decomposition improves the TSR of tightness-biased models on sensitive tasks by up to 15.8%, and reduces the attack success rate on all tested models.

### Limitations and future work

The paper's limitations are that the policy language only covers file-level read/write/execute permissions in terminal tasks, not network access, IPC, database credentials, cloud resources, browser state, or API-level permissions; reasoning intensity analysis is limited to model families with configurable reasoning levels. The evaluation covers 120 terminal tasks and 9 frontier models, and does not report other non-file-system permissions.

## CORVUS: Using LoRA to disguise internal signals and bypass single-forward hallucination detectors

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14310) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/ex6mx30sytmmqyhec3xtgxalb)

Researchers from Singapore Management University and other institutions propose CORVUS, a white-box model-side red-teaming method: freezing the detector and base model, using only a LoRA adapter under teacher forcing to reshape three types of internal telemetry signals in the answer window—token entropy (TE), hidden-state log-volume (HV), and attention diagonality (AD)—and adding a one-step embedding-space FGSM attention stress test. The adapter is trained on only 1,000 out-of-distribution Alpaca instructions with fewer than 0.5% trainable parameters, then transfers zero-shot to FAVA-Annotation, simultaneously weakening both training-free detectors (e.g., LLM-Check) and probe-based detectors (SEP, ICR-probe) across four open-source models: Llama-2, Vicuna, Llama-3, and Qwen2.5. Ablations show that the AD term is the main lever for evading attention-based features, with TE and HV providing secondary shaping.

### Questions

Single-pass hallucination detectors rely on the model's internal computational telemetry (such as uncertainty, hidden state geometry, and attention). Previous evaluations assumed the model is passive and did not consider the risk that, in open-weight or self-hosted deployments, a model-side adversary could evade detection by modifying the model's internal representations.

### Methods

The authors propose the CORVUS method, which freezes the base model and trains a lightweight LoRA adapter. Under teacher forcing, it directly optimizes the token entropy and hidden log-volume of the answer tokens, and introduces an adversarial attention diagonality loss via single-step embedding-space FGSM perturbation, camouflaging internal telemetry while keeping the surface answer unchanged.

### Experiments and results

The adapter is trained on 1,000 Alpaca instructions and then zero-shot transferred to FAVA-Annotation. The authors report that it reduces the performance of training-free detectors and probes on Llama-2, Vicuna, Llama-3, and Qwen2.5, with attention score AUROC dropping to 48.08 on Llama-2.

The paper proposes Confidence-Weighted Persuasion Override Rate (CW-POR) to measure how often a judge LLM is persuaded by incorrect arguments in multi-agent debate and how confident it is in the wrong option. The study uses a single-round multi-agent debate framework: one LLM-based agent gives the truthful answer from TruthfulQA, another agent argues for the incorrect claim, and the same LLM architecture serves as judge. Experiments cover five open-source LLMs (3B to 14B parameters) and systematically vary agent utterance length (30 to 300 words). Results show that even smaller models can construct persuasive arguments that override truthful answers, often with high confidence. The authors argue this highlights the need for robust calibration and adversarial testing to prevent LLMs from confidently endorsing misinformation.

### Questions

When an LLM both generates and judges, neutral facts and forceful incorrect statements seen in a single round may conflict. The authors aim to measure together how often the judge is persuaded and how confident it is when choosing wrong.

### Methods

On the TruthfulQA validation split, the same model family plays the roles of neutral explanation, erroneous defense, and judge. The judge reads both sides randomly and scores 1–5. CW-POR weights incorrect choices using normalized self-assessment multiplied by LLC, with lengths ranging from 30 to 300 words.

### Experiments and results

Across five 3B–14B models, the authors claim that confident misjudgment is least likely around 90–120 words, with trends diverging after longer lengths. Most models have higher CW-POR on non-adversarial questions; Phi-4 14B still shows high textual confidence when choosing incorrectly. The main text does not include specific percentages.

### Limitations and future work

The authors propose expanding categories with high CW-POR and few samples, adding limited rebuttals, using judges of different architectures, and applying thresholds or external verification when combined confidence is high. This run covers five models, triangular same-family, single-round; the paper does not report the number of questions or repetitions.

## MemoAttack: Memory-driven black-box jailbreak framework achieves 93.33-96.67% attack success rate on AdvBench

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29237) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/a4305k1zl1zz6f6wur8xgaks4)

The researchers propose MemoAttack, a memory-driven black-box jailbreak framework that organizes and reuses attack experience through skill-structured memory modeling, lifecycle-driven memory evolution, and posterior-guided contextual memory selection. On AdvBench against three target models, MemoAttack achieves an attack success rate of 93.33-96.67%, outperforming the strongest baseline on each target by 10.00-12.00 percentage points, while reducing the average expansion cost on its own successful targets by 20.2-51.6%. In a sequential evaluation of 400 targets on Qwen3.5, as memory accumulates, the average number of expansion attempts for the last 50 targets drops from 19.74 to 10.92. The paper is marked as under review and belongs to the cs.CR area.

### Questions

Aligned LLMs should refuse harmful requests, but under black-box conditions the attacker only sees outputs, and automatic jailbreaking is sparse and expensive search. Sample-level heuristic search has short-lived experience; cross-sample policy pools lack structured management.

### Methods

MemoAttack stores experience as skill memories with templates, evidence, and lifecycle. The controller samples among reuse, mutation, and invention, uses two Beta posteriors for progress and success plus contextual features for Thompson sampling, and shows only a shortlist projection to the attacker.

### Experiments and results

On AdvBench, ASR for kimi-k2.5, minimax-M2.1, and qwen3.5 is 95.33%, 96.67%, and 93.33%. Removing memory from Qwen3.5 causes the largest drop; tail expansion count decreases from 19.74 to 10.92.

### Limitations and future work

The authors note that evaluation is limited to AdvBench text black-box and three targets, does not evaluate Claude Fable 5 or GPT-5.6, and does not systematically study cross-target transfer, hyperparameter sensitivity, or defenses. SD comes from three non-overlapping splits; the paper states that confidence intervals are not computed.

## Prompt-in-Content attacks: hijacking LLM behavior using uploaded input

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19287) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/j0fqo9ja5efsxofgb0kinu9lb)

The paper proposes a new class of attack called prompt in content injection, which embeds adversarial instructions in seemingly benign uploaded documents or pasted text. After the LLM processes them, it can generate biased summaries, fabricated claims, or misleading advice, without user awareness and without the system being compromised. The authors verified the feasibility of the attack on multiple mainstream platforms, analyzed its root cause as insufficient prompt concatenation and input isolation, and discussed mitigation strategies.

### Questions

Commercial LLMs often concatenate the uploaded document and the user query into the same prompt. A third party can embed instructions in a seemingly normal file, and when the user performs an ordinary task such as summarization, the model may rewrite, refuse, or deflect the output without the user's knowledge.

### Methods

The authors define the prompt-in-content attack: the attacker pre-writes a single natural-language instruction in the middle of a document, without touching the model interface or using jailbreaks. The four goal types are task suppression, output replacement, behavior redirection, and framework manipulation. The evaluation uploads .docx files on the default web interfaces of seven services and observes the outputs.

### Experiments and results

The authors state that most platforms execute embedded instructions. Table 1: Grok 3, DeepSeek R1, and Kimi were successful across all four types; Claude Sonnet4 and ChatGPT 4o blocked all four types. Grok 3 also placed the previously mentioned password into a redirect link (Figure 5).

### Limitations and future work

The authors limited themselves to single-turn public web interfaces and did not examine multi-turn persistence, long-term memory leakage, or account-linked poisoning; the exfiltration test was in a controlled environment and did not use real user data. The evaluation covered seven web services with a single mid-document instruction in .docx files, did not report sample size or number of repetitions, and did not experimentally evaluate the defense recommendations in Section 5.2.

## Multi-turn jailbreak attacks on multimodal large language models and FragGuard defense

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05339) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/q91lq83i23kof7nxin0u1aegv)

The paper proposes the MJAD-MLLMs framework, systematically analyzing multi-turn jailbreak attacks and multi-LLM-based defense techniques for multimodal large language models (MLLMs). The authors design a new multi-turn jailbreak attack that exploits model vulnerabilities under multi-turn prompts, and propose a fragment optimization and multi-LLM defense mechanism, FragGuard, to mitigate such attacks. The study evaluates the attack and defense effectiveness on multiple SOTA open-source and closed-source MLLMs and benchmark datasets, and compares with existing techniques.

### Questions

The basic guardrails of MLLMs may be bypassed by multi-turn multimodal prompts. Multi-turn jailbreak research exists on the text side, but systematic analysis is lacking on the MLLM side; the authors believe existing defenses may be insufficient against multi-turn jailbreaks.

### Methods

The framework MJAD-MLLMs induces jailbreak through three rounds of dialogue and bottom-aligned images. FragGuard splits responses at 400 tokens, uses o1, Gemini-2.5-Flash-lite, and LLaMa-3 (70B) to take the maximum toxicity score, and if it exceeds the threshold, it changes to a safe refusal.

### Experiments and results

LLaVa-7B and GPT-4o have Turn3 ASR of 91.50% and 77.31% (Table 1). After FragGuard, they are 0.96% and 8.08% (Table 3). The authors state that Gemini-2.0-Flash has an ASR of 82.3%.

### Limitations and future work

The authors plan to automatically generate adversarial prompts and analyze defenses for commercial and reasoning MLLMs and automated attacks. Experiments use MM-SafetyBench with 40 items per category; Table 3 only includes two models. The paper does not report threshold τ and repetition count.

## MARGIN: Runtime Confidence Calibration for Multi-Agent Foundation Model Coordination

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22949) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/a1f0isxzej9r2coyxwuuduetd)

The paper proposes MARGIN (Multi-Agent Runtime Grading via Incremental Normalisation), a runtime calibration method that learns model-specific confidence corrections from observed answer outcomes without retraining models or a held-out calibration set. In code generation, question answering, and math evaluations on an 18-model pool and a 9-model subset, MARGIN achieved lower post-transfer expected calibration error than all five online calibration baselines on two code generation transfers, and lower than four on the question answering transfer. In code generation coordination experiments, calibration improved answer selection accuracy on two benchmarks by 4.3 and 14.0 percentage points, respectively.

### Questions

The coordinator needs to compare answers from heterogeneous base models using self-reported confidence; the same score means different things across models, and after the task distribution changes the old correction may also no longer apply. The authors argue this becomes a coordination problem, not just a single-model diagnostic.

### Methods

Within each model's confidence bands, MARGIN tracks accuracy and self-reported confidence with an EWMA, corrects scores by their ratio, and shrinks sparse bands toward that model's overall ratio. The corrected scores serve as voting weights for the answers already obtained. No retraining, no access to internal state, no separate calibration set, but it depends on subsequent correctness feedback.

### Experiments and results

After two code transfers, nine-cloud MARGIN achieves ECE of 12.99 and 11.89, lower than the five online baselines; MMLU-shift is 2.85, inconclusive compared with online Platt. The 18-model cold-start selection accuracy improves by 4.32 and 13.98 percentage points on MBPP and BCB.

### Limitations and future work

The authors state that applicability depends on correctness feedback; verifying only the selected answerer harms calibration; generalization is limited by the archived queue and mutation transfer, and gradual drift was not tested. The experiments use an 18-model pool and a nine-cloud subset; question answering and math only do calibration, not voting.
